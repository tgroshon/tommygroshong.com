# Health, Safety, and Liberty

Normies, boomers, and statists alike often point to health and safety
regulations as a cornerstone of why we need The Guberment. "Who will protect us
from evil corporations poisoning our food with arsenic?" they ask, implying only
government agencies can save us. No doubt plenty of people WOULD poison you or I
for the right profit, but the astute observer of reality would discern that such
persons exist on both sides of the corporate-political divide. 

The difference is a corporate manager would poison you for money while the
government bureacrat would poison you simply to avoid the inconvenience of more
work.

The reality of these government policy managed regulations (after all, they're
rarely backed up with ACTUAL LAWS) is that they do little to protect people,
massively inflate the cost of doing business, and most commonly shield the
culpable from the victim. In most ways, the system is utterly broken—punishing
the innocent with red tape while letting corporate wrongdoers off with a
finger-wagging and financial slap on the wrist. 

There’s a better way, one that’s simpler, fairer, and doesn’t rely on
overbearing and incompetent bureacrats.

## The Problems

The current regulatory environment is a bureaucratic nightmare. Small businesses
and startups are crushed under the same compliance burdens as megacorporations,
which have armies of lawyers and accountants to navigate the red tape. More
often than not, regulatory agencies instituted policies _at the behest_ of large
incumbents as an artificial barrier to their competitors. Meanwhile, when actual
harm occurs—like a company knowingly using substandard processes or ingredients
that cause real harm like illness or death—the punishment is laughably
inadequate if it occurs at all. A pharmaceutical giant poisons thousands? They
pay a fine—say, $100 million on a drug that raked in billions—and move on. No
one goes to jail. No one faces real consequences. So predictable it’s become a
dark joke amongst the common folk.

This system clearly fails on two fronts: it overburdens the honest and
underpunishes the guilty. It’s not about safety; it’s about control and
protecting entrenched interests. A better approach would prioritize real
accountability, minimize barriers for small players, and rely on common law and
private mechanisms rather than state-run regulatory agencies.

## A Three-Pronged Solution

Consider the following framework for health and safety that delivers justice
without the bloat:

### 1. Treat Harm as a Crime, Not a Fine

Intentional or negligent actions that cause death or injury should be prosecuted
as violent crimes: e.g. manslaughter, murder, or assault. If a company knowingly
uses toxic ingredients, the individuals responsible (not just the corporate
entity) should face legal action: specifically, arrest and trial, no different
from a street criminal. Expensive suits and corner offices don’t change the act. 

Negligence is no excuse either. If a company claims, "We didn’t know the
ingredient was dangerous," but fails to ensure safety, that’s still manslaughter
if someone dies, or aggravated assault if someone’s injured. A reckless driver
who kills or maims someone in an accidental crash: it's not intentional murder,
but it's negligent homicide. Executives and decision-makers, from the VP
approving the email chain to the manager overseeing production, should face
personal liability, be charged, investigated, and defend themselves in court. 

And like any violent felony, jail time, not fines, for those convicted.

### 2. Open the Door to Lawsuits

Any negative side effect from a product should be grounds for lawsuits and tort
claims. The blanket protections shielding pharmaceutical companies from
liability when they roll out new drugs are absurdly unjust. If a product harms
someone, victims should have clear recourse to sue for damages. This creates a
natural incentive for companies to prioritize safety, without needing a bloated
regulatory state to micromanage every step.

### 3. Enforce Clear Disclosures and Labeling

Transparent labeling is a no-brainer. Consumers deserve to know what’s in their
food, drugs, or cosmetics. Punish lies harshly, but don’t overcomplicate it with
government mandates. Private systems, like kosher certification, prove you can
maintain high standards without state involvement. These certifications are
rigorous, trusted, and entirely voluntary—showing that markets can handle this
better than bureaucrats.

## Why This Works

This approach is astronomically simpler and cheaper than the current mess. It
levels the playing field for small businesses, holds wrongdoers accountable, and
empowers consumers through transparency and legal recourse. Most importantly, it
doesn’t require a sprawling state apparatus. Common law courts and private
enterprise can handle these mechanisms, passing the libertarian litmus test: if
it needs a state to exist, it’s probably wrong.

## A Practical Path Forward

I’m no naive libertarian calling for the overnight dismantling of all government
agencies. Without existing private structures, that’s a pipe dream. But any
proposed system should pass the test of whether it *could* function without a
state. This one can. Even a statist society adopting these principles would be
more just and effective than what we have now. It’s time to stop letting
corporations hide behind fines and bureaucrats hide behind red tape. Let’s
demand real accountability and real freedom.
